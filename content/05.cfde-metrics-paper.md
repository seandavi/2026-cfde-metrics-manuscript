## Billions invested, no common yardstick

Public biomedical data resources have become central infrastructure for biology, medicine, and the data sciences.
NIH alone has invested billions over the past decade in initiatives that include The Cancer Genome Atlas [@tcga2013pancancer], the Genotype-Tissue Expression project [@doi:10.1038/ng.2653], the All of Us Research Program [@allofus2019], and the 19 programs of the Common Fund.
Whether any given resource is succeeding goes mostly unanswered.
Each funded program tracks its own metrics in its own way, which blocks portfolio-level comparison and the evidence-driven funding decisions that comparison enables.

We argue that a funder must answer three questions about any data resource: is anyone better off (*public value*), can the data be trusted and reused (*data quality and reusability*), and can the resource keep running (*operations and finance*).
We expect the relative weight of these three domains to shift across a resource's lifecycle.
Uniform weighting across all phases risks undervaluing infrastructure-building: early-phase investment in data quality and reusability looks expensive when the comparison metric is mid-phase public value.

The NIH **Common Fund Data Ecosystem (CFDE)** (see Glossary) [@doi:10.1093/gigascience/giac105] is a cross-cutting initiative that harmonizes metadata and tooling across the 19 Common Fund programs.
The Council of Councils Working Group identified "facilitation of new scientific discovery" as the chief metric of CFDE success ^i^.
We use the CFDE as motivating case study and operational testbed, and describe the **Integration and Coordination Center's (ICC)** first portfolio-wide dashboard, which operationalizes the framework in near real time.

## Three domains, one lifecycle

None of the three domains is new; the existing literature on evaluating research data and infrastructure treats each of them, using different groupings.
Repository-trust frameworks, the **TRUST principles** [@doi:10.1038/s41597-020-0486-7] and the **CoreTrustSeal** certification requirements [@doi:10.5281/zenodo.17660463], concentrate on stewardship and continuity: curation and quality control, funding, governance, and technical infrastructure.
The indicators used to select ELIXIR Core Data Resources [@doi:10.12688/f1000research.9656.2] and Global Core Biodata Resources [@doi:10.5281/zenodo.7468719] fall into five categories: scientific quality, community served, quality of service, funding and governance, and impact.
Impact frameworks for research infrastructures, such as those of the OECD and the RI-PATHS project, look beyond scientific impact to economic, societal, and wider public value [@doi:10.12688/f1000research.108804.2], and perceived-value models built on information-systems success and technology-acceptance theory ask what users gain [@doi:10.2196/63544].
Mapped onto one another, these dimensions fall mostly into three groups: community served and impact are *public value*; scientific quality is *data quality and reusability*; quality of service, funding, and governance are *operations and finance*.

Three domains are few enough for a funder to weigh against one another in a portfolio decision, and each answers a distinct question about a resource.
Cross-cutting concerns are placed by the question their evidence answers: governance under *operations and finance* and community engagement under *public value*, although both also shape the other domains.

The domains overlap and sometimes conflict (more scientifically impactful datasets are typically more expensive to create and maintain), and weighted composite scoring such as **Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA)** can balance them when needed [@isbn:9780471465102; @doi:10.1007/978-1-4615-1495-4].
We propose phase-dependent priorities.
In the **early phase (Infrastructure & Collection)**, *data quality and reusability* and *operations and finance* dominate.
In the **mid phase (Active Use & Impact)**, all three matter, with *public value* taking on increasing importance.
In the **late phase (Sustainability & Legacy)**, *operations and finance* and *public value* become the primary lenses (@fig-lifecycle_curves).
Uniform metric panels across phases risk misallocating funding; we propose lifecycle-aware weighting as an organizing principle for evaluation, not a refinement on top.

These phases describe the lifecycle of the resource as infrastructure, not the lifecycle of a dataset.
Research data management models such as the DCC Curation Lifecycle Model [@doi:10.2218/ijdc.v3i1.48] and the ELIXIR RDMkit [@doi:10.1016/j.patter.2025.101345] describe data management and curation lifecycles, from planning and collection through preservation, sharing, and reuse; a resource runs those cycles many times, for many datasets, within each of its own phases.
The phases overlap, and activities from one continue into the next, but two events serve as practical markers: the first public release of data or services marks the move from early to mid, and a funding transition, such as the end of build-out funding or a move to a sustainment or renewal award, often marks the move to late.
Late does not mean unfunded; it means the emphasis has shifted from build-out toward sustaining access.
The CFDE shows the pattern: Common Fund programs are time-limited, lasting 10 years or less ^ii^, so each program moves from build-out into sustainment.

The weights in @fig-lifecycle_curves are a normative proposal informed by our experience coordinating the CFDE, not an empirical estimate, and we offer them as a testable hypothesis.
Testing them needs two kinds of evidence, and neither alone sets the weights.
Weights elicited from funders, program staff, and resource managers at each phase would show whether stakeholders agree with them; longitudinal portfolio data would show whether phase-dependent weighting predicts specified outcomes, such as later reuse and survival after build-out funding ends, better than uniform weighting.

The *public value* domain captures outputs and impacts: use of the resource and its data, citations, and downstream scientific influence.
The *data quality and reusability* domain covers the intrinsic quality of the data and the conditions for their reuse, the latter guided by the **FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable)** [@doi:10.1038/sdata.2016.18; @doi:10.1038/sdata.2018.118].
The *operations and finance* domain covers infrastructure reliability and cost sustainability, including robustness as funding sunsets.
We propose three core indicators per domain (@tbl-core-indicators), argued for below with their limits; Supplementary Table S1 defines the full catalog.

### Public value

Website engagement metrics (*page views*, *time on page*, *actions/triggers*, *new vs. returning users*) are easy to collect via platforms such as Google Analytics or Matomo, but they capture interest rather than actual data use.
More direct evidence comes from *downloads* and *compute jobs*, which together form the core indicator *data use events*; cloud-hosted datasets let analysis activity be measured rather than inferred.
Limitations are persistent: bots skew counts, VPN use confounds new-versus-returning tracking, and downloads cannot distinguish active use from passive acquisition.
As AI-driven and agent-based traffic on data portals grows — Cloudflare data show training-purpose AI crawler traffic rising rapidly across 2024 and 2025 [@belson2025aicrawlers] — the bot signal will need to be partitioned into noise to remove and demand to interpret.

Citations give stronger proxies for scientific impact, but come in two kinds.
*Platform citations* are citations to papers describing the resource; *content citations* are citations to, or reuse of, the data it hosts.
The original GTEx paper [@doi:10.1038/ng.2653] had accumulated 8,630 platform citations as of January 2026.
Platform citations are easy to collect from PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, Clarivate, and OpenAlex and complement content citations as a measure of visibility, but they mix use of the data with mentions of the resource and favor large fields and older resources; content citations, which tie a publication to data the resource hosts, are the more direct evidence of reuse.
*Resource publications* on technical development and maintenance are a qualified documentation proxy under *operations and finance*, not proof of maturity or sustainability; the scientific contributions a resource enables count as *public value*.
Composite scores that add second-generation citations (citations to the citing papers) capture cumulative downstream impact that simple counts miss, and citation networks reveal cross-disciplinary collaborations enabled by data sharing; successful sharing also produces a citation boost for the original data generators [@doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0311493].
Citation counts lag actual use by months to years [@doi:10.1038/520429a], and journal-scoring alternatives that weight by citing-paper properties better capture subject-specific repository impact than raw counts [@doi:10.4103/1008-682X.171582].
The deeper problem is the citation gap: fewer than 30% of secondary analyses formally cite their source data [@doi:10.1093/bioinformatics/btad746].
Standard bibliometrics therefore systematically understate reuse, and programs that lean on citation counts as their primary impact key performance indicator (KPI) are measuring reach rather than influence.
Closing the gap requires data ecosystems to mine accession numbers (e.g., GEO accession numbers, NCBI BioProject IDs) from publication full text rather than rely on citation databases.
A bibliometric analysis of NeuroMorpho.Org found that shared neural morphology data sustained a reuse rate constant for sixteen years and effectively doubled peer-reviewed discoveries in the field [@doi:10.1093/bioinformatics/btad746].

Two indicators often reported as public value are weak proxies, so we move them to Supplementary Table S1.
A subsequent grant that builds on a resource shows that researchers won further funding, not that anyone else benefited, and attributing grants to a resource relies on incomplete self-reporting.
Presentations by the resource team measure its dissemination effort rather than benefit to others; presentations of independent work based on the resource are closer to reuse but rarely recorded.

Openness is distinct from FAIR, which asks for a clear usage license and allows authenticated access where necessary [@doi:10.1038/sdata.2016.18].
*License type*, *access model* (open, registered, controlled, or invitation-only), and *regulatory compliance support*, such as resource-specific plan templates for the NIH **Data Management and Sharing Policy (DMS Policy)** ^iii^, shape who can benefit and can change as a resource matures.
They stay in Supplementary Table S1 rather than the core set because they are context for reading use counts, not measures of value: controlled access can suppress downloads without implying lower scientific value.

Community size is a second such context.
Absolute counts of visits, downloads, and citations favor general-purpose resources serving many fields over domain-specific resources that may be indispensable to a small community.
We therefore treat community scope not as a separate domain but as a normalizing factor across *public value*: counts should be read relative to the size of the intended community, much as the **Relative Citation Ratio (RCR)** normalizes an article's citations by the rate expected for its field [@doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.1002541].
Candidate denominators, such as field publication volume, need pilot validation, with matched numerator, denominator, and time window, absolute counts alongside, and sensitivity analyses, since general-purpose resources serve overlapping fields.

User-centered measures of *perceived value* offer complementary insight.
Work integrating the Information System Success Model, the Technology Acceptance Model, and Consumer Perceived Value Theory identifies four entropy-weighted metrics that explain the majority of perceived value: *number of datasets*, *data timeliness*, *search comprehensiveness*, and *system responsiveness* [@doi:10.2196/63544].

The long-term measure of public value is *practice change* — a shift from hypothesis-driven research toward data-driven discovery, and from evidence-based practice toward practice-based evidence [@doi:10.15265/IY-2014-0020].
Concrete indicators include the cloud-versus-local analysis ratio, cross-disciplinary co-authorship across Common Fund programs, and the geographic and institutional breadth of the user base, especially participation by early-career investigators from under-resourced institutions.
Until practice change is measurable, every other public value metric is at best a leading indicator.
Use and citations can be counted; whether that use changed what researchers did, and why, takes user case studies, interviews, and the impact stories that ELIXIR and the Global Biodata Coalition ask resources to supply [@doi:10.12688/f1000research.9656.2; @doi:10.5281/zenodo.7468719].

### Data quality and reusability

Poor data quality and poor annotation are the leading barriers to reuse [@doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0229003].

Trust and reuse depend on different properties: *intrinsic quality* (validity, accuracy, provenance, and the quality control (QC) and curation applied) determines whether data are scientifically sound, and *reusability* (FAIR compliance, metadata, and documentation) determines whether others can find and use them.
A dataset can be fully FAIR and well documented and still hold poor measurements, and most indicators in this domain measure reusability: they can show that QC was performed, that provenance is recorded, and that documentation lets users judge fitness for use, but not that the measurements are valid.
The CoreTrustSeal requirements draw the same line, separating a repository's "technical quality" from the "scientific quality" of the data it holds [@doi:10.5281/zenodo.17660463].
Judging intrinsic quality takes domain-expert review against community QC standards; the core indicator *QC compliance* can record that such review happened but cannot replace it.
Metadata completeness, schema validity, and automated FAIR tests are quantitative; representativeness and intrinsic quality combine quantitative checks with expert interpretation, and data-dictionary usefulness also requires qualitative assessment.

Metadata spans study, data, and dataset levels, and summary metrics across them are more tractable for portfolio evaluation than cataloging every study-specific variable.
The Standard Evaluation Framework for Large Health Care Data identifies 49 attributes across three domains (collection process, data, and data use) for assessing fitness for purpose [@doi:10.15585/mmwr.su7303a1].
Two of those attributes are particularly load-bearing for federated ecosystems: *representativeness*, the extent to which a dataset generalizes beyond its source population (critical for avoiding both Type 1 and Type 2 errors in scientific conclusions drawn from the data, as the European-ancestry bias in human-genetics studies has repeatedly demonstrated [@doi:10.1016/j.cell.2019.02.048; @doi:10.1038/538161a]), and *linkability*, the extent to which data integrate with external sources — a paramount concern for an ecosystem whose mission is cross-program query.

For scientific software, quality follows from open-source development practices: *README* and *LICENSE* files, automated testing [@doi:10.1038/nbt.3780], and inclusion in curated ecosystems like Bioconductor [@doi:10.1186/gb-2004-5-10-r80].
The **ELIXIR** "Top 10 Metrics for Life Science Software Good Practices" provides a prioritized scorecard spanning version control, discoverability, automated builds, test data, and documentation [@doi:10.12688/f1000research.9206.1].
**PIsCO (Performance Indicators framework for Collection of bioinformatics resource metrics)** automates server-side collection and visualization of these metrics so they can flow directly into evaluation dashboards [@doi:10.12688/f1000research.9206.1].

A structured assessment of overall quality comes from FAIR maturity models.
The shift from first-generation FAIR metrics (manually assessed) to second-generation maturity indicators (automated and machine-measurable) [@doi:10.1038/sdata.2018.118] is essential for the CFDE, where the volume of metadata prevents manual audit.
The **FAIRplus Dataset Maturity (FAIRplus-DSM)** model ^iv^, developed for the life sciences, defines six levels, from Level 0 (no identifiers, local storage) to Level 5 (cross-community provenance and enterprise-grade lifecycle management).
The **Research Data Alliance (RDA)** further classifies FAIR indicators as Essential (globally unique identifiers, open protocols), Important (machine-understandable knowledge representation), or Useful (automated agent access), giving evaluators a tiered roadmap.
Automated tools such as the FAIR Evaluator test resources at scale via Compliance Tests, making continuous *FAIR compliance* monitoring feasible for large portfolios [@doi:10.1038/sdata.2018.118].
The core *automated FAIR maturity score* tests machine-actionable properties whose absence blocks discovery, access, and reuse, not intrinsic quality, and should be reported with the tool and test set used.

Certifications and endorsements give an external signal of maturity, but they assess different things.
CoreTrustSeal certification, valid for three years, is a peer-reviewed self-assessment of a repository's curation, preservation, governance, funding, and technical infrastructure [@doi:10.5281/zenodo.17660463], and the TRUST principles treat such certification as evidence of a repository's investment in its own trustworthiness [@doi:10.1038/s41597-020-0486-7].
Selection as a Global Core Biodata Resource follows review against quantitative and qualitative indicators of scientific quality, community, service, governance, and impact [@doi:10.5281/zenodo.7468719]; inclusion in the NIH-maintained list of domain-specific data repositories ^v^ is a directory listing rather than an assessment against published criteria.
None of these signals is evidence of intrinsic data quality, and each can stand in only for the criteria its scheme actually assessed, so evaluators should record the scheme, scope, and date rather than a single endorsement score.

### Operations and finance

Server-level metrics (*uptime*, *page load time*, *latency*, *server errors*, *client errors*) and compute-level metrics (*download speed*, *cpu/gpu usage*, *memory usage*, *client queue times*, *job run times*) are easy to capture but carry day-to-day variability from load, job size, and client-side network conditions, so trend monitoring is more informative than point-in-time values.
Of these, *uptime* and *server errors* are the core indicators: a resource that is unavailable or failing delivers no value.
For open-source projects, GitHub repository analytics provide an additional operational window: development velocity, signals of technical debt (stale pull requests, unresolved issues), and contributor dynamics that separate core-team commits from external contributions and so register whether training and outreach are broadening adoption.

The Council of Councils identified sustainability as the "critical issue" for the CFDE, primarily because the programs that generate data are finite in duration ^i^.
*Funding cost* is a core indicator but harder to establish than it looks: award totals rarely give the full cost, because a resource may draw on several awards, institutional and in-kind support, and cloud credits, and its funding model changes over its life.
A complete picture decomposes it into *sample collection*, *sample measurement*, *QC*, *data storage*, *data preservation* (beyond the grant period), *computing hardware*, *server maintenance*, *cloud-based computing*, and *personnel*.
The National Academies six-step framework for forecasting biomedical information-resource costs identifies cost-driver categories — data content, metadata, labor, infrastructure, compliance, governance — and notes that labor (curation and user support) consistently represents the single largest cost element ^vi^.
Compliance costs in particular (HIPAA, FISMA, regulatory governance for human-subjects data such as Kids First and GTEx) are often underestimated in initial budgeting, and cloud-computing costs add provider-specific, usage-dependent volatility that resists multi-year forecasting [@doi:10.1093/jamia/ocab278].

The harder sustainability question is the break-even point for data sharing.
Curation costs are borne by data generators while reuse benefits accrue elsewhere — an asymmetry that means aggregate gains can fail to cover aggregate costs even when both are positive.
A recent scoping review of open-science economics catalogs substantial measured value across efficiency, innovation, and growth from open data and software, though most studies estimate aggregate return on investment rather than the marginal break-even at the dataset level [@doi:10.1098/rsos.250754].
This argues for selective investment in high-value datasets rather than uniform support across the portfolio.
*Cost per reuse* — curation and hosting cost over a stated period, excluding build-out, divided by reuse in that period — gives a concrete KPI for portfolio-level decisions.
File packaging and job splitting change event counts without changing reuse, so each reuse unit needs its own ratio, such as cost per independent reusing publication or per distinct user-project; ratios compare across resources only under matched cost scope and counting rules, and the publication ratio inherits the citation gap.
Uptime, latency, and cost are quantitative; whether governance, staffing, and succession plans can carry a resource past the end of its funding is a qualitative judgment, which CoreTrustSeal makes by peer review of documented evidence [@doi:10.5281/zenodo.17660463].

## Implementation in practice

The CFDE Integration and Coordination Center (ICC) operates a portfolio-wide dashboard that demonstrates this framework is implementable.
The ICC currently collects a prioritized subset of the full catalog (@tbl-metrics-cfde), drawing on NIH-provided structural data (NIH CFDE Website, NIH RePORTER) for funding and project context, PubMed and NIH iCite (including the RCR) for publication and citation impact, GitHub for repository use, quality, and operations across the open-source software components, and Google Analytics for website engagement.
Project teams onboard by labeling GitHub repositories with their NIH core project number (for example, "R01AG123456") and granting the ICC a Google Analytics service account, with NIH program staff supporting the trust and access conversation.

## Concluding remarks

Standard metrics carry stakes: once adopted, they can justify or deny funding.
The community they will judge (data-sharing, FAIR, and repository leaders, funders, and resource operators) should therefore agree which indicators to use, how to collect them, and how to weight them, and the lifecycle weights in @fig-lifecycle_curves are exactly what that process should test.
Four steps lead from this conceptual framework to practice: a structured consensus process on core indicators, their definitions, and initial phase weights, starting from @tbl-core-indicators; a prototype that pilots the agreed set across resource types, from general-purpose to small-community, and across lifecycle phases; shared collection infrastructure at scale, with common counting rules such as the Code of Practice for Research Data Usage Metrics [@doi:10.7287/peerj.preprints.26505v1], open tooling, and a common reporting format; and periodic re-weighting, testing phase-dependent against uniform weights on later reuse and survival after build-out funding ends.

The CFDE already prototypes parts of this path: its Metrics Working Group plays the role that **GA4GH's** "Driver Projects" model has demonstrated elsewhere ^viii^, iterating on a small priority set of metrics with NIH program staff and cross-center representatives before expanding portfolio-wide.
Each domain also needs qualitative evidence, as the NIH Common Fund's "360-degree view" of program performance recognizes ^ix^.
Mixed-method designs explain *why* resources succeed or fail, not just *what* is happening [@doi:10.1007/s10488-013-0528-y; @doi:10.1002/jgf2.736], and logic models ^x^ help identify hypothesized critical paths to success through a portfolio [@pmid:30861395].

The harder challenge is closing the "know-do gap" between what metrics show and actual decision-making [@pmid:16283043].
Evidence from health care suggests that repeated, structured measurement itself drives behavioral change, a Hawthorne effect [@doi:10.1016/j.socscimed.2017.03.041], and NIH already requires regular FAIRness assessments and usage reporting ^iii^; without pre-policy baselines captured now, the effect of the 2023 DMS Policy on sharing and reuse can be inferred but not measured.
What this paper proposes is not a final metric set but a shared evaluation language for federated biomedical data ecosystems, and an invitation to test it together (see "Outstanding questions").
